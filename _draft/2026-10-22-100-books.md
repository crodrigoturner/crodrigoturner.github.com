I asked Claude for a list of books everyone should read, then updated it with books I want to read. I'll link to my notes as I read them. [Have a recommendation? ](/contact)


1. **The Epic of Gilgamesh** — Anonymous — The oldest surviving great story: a king's search for immortality and his acceptance that he will die.
2. **The Iliad** — Homer — Rage, honour and grief during the final weeks of the Trojan War.
3. **The Odyssey** — Homer — A clever veteran's ten-year journey home, and the model for every adventure story since.
4. **Oedipus Rex** — Sophocles — A king's investigation into a plague that leads back to himself.
5. **The Histories** — Herodotus — The first work of history: the Persian Wars, told through travel, myth and inquiry.
6. **The Republic** — Plato — What justice is, and what a just city and a just soul would look like.
7. **Nicomachean Ethics** — Aristotle — Happiness as a lifetime of practised virtue, not a feeling.
8. **The Art of War** — Sun Tzu — Strategy in short maxims: win by knowing yourself, your opponent and the terrain.
9. **Tao Te Ching** — Laozi — Short verses on simplicity, humility and acting by not forcing.
10. **The Analects** — Confucius — Sayings on character, duty and how a good society is held together.
11. **The Bhagavad Gita** — Anonymous — A dialogue on a battlefield about duty, action and letting go of results.
12. <mark>**Meditations** — Marcus Aurelius — A Roman emperor's private notes to himself on staying calm, fair and humble.</mark>
13. **One Thousand and One Nights** — Anonymous — Scheherazade tells stories to survive, with stories nested inside stories.
14. **The Divine Comedy** — Dante Alighieri — A journey through Hell, Purgatory and Paradise that maps the whole medieval world.
15. **The Prince** — Niccolò Machiavelli — A cold, practical handbook on how power is won and kept.
16. **Essays** — Michel de Montaigne — The man who invented the essay by honestly studying himself.
17. **Hamlet** — William Shakespeare — A prince who thinks too much about death, revenge and what is real.
18. **Discourse on the Method** — René Descartes — Doubt everything, then rebuild knowledge from "I think, therefore I am."
19. **Gulliver's Travels** — Jonathan Swift — Satire disguised as a travel book, mocking human pride at every scale.
20. <mark>**Candide** — Voltaire — A comic demolition of the idea that everything happens for the best.</mark>
21. <mark>**The Wealth of Nations** — Adam Smith — How specialisation, trade and self-interest make nations prosperous.</mark>
22. <mark>**Pride and Prejudice** — Jane Austen — First impressions, class and money, in the sharpest comedy of manners.</mark>
23. **Frankenstein** — Mary Shelley — The first science-fiction novel: a creator who abandons what he created.
24. **Democracy in America** — Alexis de Tocqueville — A Frenchman's study of the strengths and dangers of democracy.
25. **The Count of Monte Cristo** — Alexandre Dumas — A wrongly imprisoned man's long, patient revenge.
26. **Jane Eyre** — Charlotte Brontë — An orphan who refuses to give up her self-respect for love or security.
27. **Moby-Dick** — Herman Melville — One captain's obsession with a white whale, and everything else about the sea.
28. **Walden** — Henry David Thoreau — Two years in a cabin, testing how little a good life really needs.
29. **On the Origin of Species** — Charles Darwin — Natural selection, the idea that explains all of life.
30. **On Liberty** — John Stuart Mill — Why society should limit its power over the individual.
31. **Great Expectations** — Charles Dickens — An orphan's rise, and what money and status do to character.
32. **Les Misérables** — Victor Hugo — Justice, mercy and redemption through one man's life in 19th-century France.
33. **Crime and Punishment** — Fyodor Dostoevsky — A student commits murder to prove a theory, and conscience takes over.
34. **Thus Spoke Zarathustra** — Friedrich Nietzsche — A prophet announces the death of God and calls people to create their own values.
35. **Heart of Darkness** — Joseph Conrad — A journey up the Congo River into the brutality of colonialism.
36. **The Metamorphosis** — Franz Kafka — A salesman wakes up as an insect, and his family shows who they really are.
37. **The Great Gatsby** — F. Scott Fitzgerald — Wealth, longing and the hollow side of the American Dream.
38. **All Quiet on the Western Front** — Erich Maria Remarque — The First World War from a young German soldier's trench.
39. **A Room of One's Own** — Virginia Woolf — The argument that creative work needs money and a room of one's own.
40. ==**Brave New World** — Aldous Huxley — A future where people are controlled through pleasure instead of fear.==
41. **The Stranger** — Albert Camus — A man condemned less for his crime than for not feeling what is expected.
42. **The Little Prince** — Antoine de Saint-Exupéry — A children's book about what adults forget to see.
43. **Ficciones** — Jorge Luis Borges — Short stories about labyrinths, infinite libraries and invented worlds.
44. ==**Animal Farm** — George Orwell — A revolution of farm animals that slowly turns into the tyranny it overthrew.==
45. ==**Man's Search for Meaning** — Viktor Frankl — What the concentration camps taught a psychiatrist about why people keep going.==
46. **The Diary of a Young Girl** — Anne Frank — A teenager's voice from two years in hiding.
47. ==**Nineteen Eighty-Four** — George Orwell — Surveillance, propaganda and the control of truth itself.==
48. **The Second Sex** — Simone de Beauvoir — How women have been defined as "the other."
49. **The Origins of Totalitarianism** — Hannah Arendt — How Nazism and Stalinism became possible.
50. **Invisible Man** — Ralph Ellison — A Black man in America whom society refuses to see.
51. **Things Fall Apart** — Chinua Achebe — An Igbo community and one proud man before and during colonial rule.
52. **To Kill a Mockingbird** — Harper Lee — Racial injustice in the American South, seen through a child's eyes.
53. **The Death and Life of Great American Cities** — Jane Jacobs — Why lively streets work and top-down planning so often fails.
54. **The Guns of August** — Barbara Tuchman — How miscalculation, pride and rigid plans led Europe into the First World War in a single month.
55. **Silent Spring** — Rachel Carson — The book about pesticides that started the environmental movement.
56. **The Structure of Scientific Revolutions** — Thomas Kuhn — Science advances through paradigm shifts, not just steady accumulation.
57. **The Fire Next Time** — James Baldwin — Two letters on race and America, as urgent as when they were written.
58. **The Autobiography of Malcolm X** — Malcolm X and Alex Haley — A life of reinvention, from prison to political leadership.
59. **The Master and Margarita** — Mikhail Bulgakov — The Devil visits Soviet Moscow in a wild satire.
60. **Slaughterhouse-Five** — Kurt Vonnegut — The firebombing of Dresden, told by a man unstuck in time.
61. **Invisible Cities** — Italo Calvino — Marco Polo describes impossible cities to Kublai Khan.
62. **The Selfish Gene** — Richard Dawkins — Evolution seen from the point of view of the gene.
63. **Gödel, Escher, Bach** — Douglas Hofstadter — How self-reference and strange loops might give rise to meaning, minds and the "I."
64. **Cosmos** — Carl Sagan — A tour of the universe and of how humans learned their place in it.
65. **The Handmaid's Tale** — Margaret Atwood — A theocracy that strips women of every freedom.
66. **A Brief History of Time** — Stephen Hawking — Black holes, the Big Bang and the nature of time, explained for everyone.
67. **The Remains of the Day** — Kazuo Ishiguro — A butler looks back on a life of loyalty and regret.
68. **The Fifth Discipline** — Peter Senge — Systems thinking applied to organisations that need to learn faster than they fail.
69. **Guns, Germs, and Steel** — Jared Diamond — Geography, crops and animals as the reasons some societies came to dominate others.
70. **A Short History of Nearly Everything** — Bill Bryson — How we know what we know, from the Big Bang to us, told with humour.
71. **Poor Charlie's Almanack** — Charlie Munger — Munger's latticework of mental models from many disciplines, used to think and invest better.
72. **Postwar** — Tony Judt — How Europe rebuilt itself from the ruins of 1945, through the Cold War, to unification.
73. **Thinking in Systems** — Donella Meadows — Stocks, flows and feedback loops: a short primer on why systems behave as they do.
74. **The Beginning of Infinity** — David Deutsch — Good explanations as the engine of progress, and why progress has no ceiling.
75. **Thinking, Fast and Slow** — Daniel Kahneman — The two systems of thought, and the biases that come with fast thinking.
76. **Why Nations Fail** — Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson — Inclusive institutions, not geography or culture, as the reason nations grow rich or stay poor.
77. **Antifragile** — Nassim Nicholas Taleb — Some things grow stronger from shocks and disorder, and how to be one of them.
78. **Sapiens** — Yuval Noah Harari — How shared stories let one species of ape take over the planet.
79. **SPQR** — Mary Beard — A thousand years of ancient Rome, from village to empire.
80. **The Silk Roads** — Peter Frankopan — World history retold from the networks that linked East and West.
81. **The Vital Question** — Nick Lane — Energy, cells and why complex life appeared only once in four billion years.
82. **Superforecasting** — Philip Tetlock and Dan Gardner — What the best forecasters do differently, and how to get better at predicting.
83. **The Gene** — Siddhartha Mukherjee — The history of the gene, from Mendel's peas to editing human DNA.
84. **Algorithms to Live By** — Brian Christian and Tom Griffiths — Lessons from computer science for everyday decisions like scheduling, searching and when to stop.
85. **Behave** — Robert Sapolsky — Why we act as we do, explained from the second before an action back to evolution.
86. **Scale** — Geoffrey West — The hidden laws that link the growth of organisms, cities and companies.
87. **The Order of Time** — Carlo Rovelli — Physics suggests that time is very different from how we experience it.
88. **Educated** — Tara Westover — A girl raised without school who ends up with a Cambridge PhD.
89. **Factfulness** — Hans Rosling — Why the world is better than we think, and the instincts that mislead us.
90. **Until the End of Time** — Brian Greene — From the Big Bang to the far future, and where mind and meaning fit in a universe that is running down.
91. **The Last Stargazers** — Emily Levesque — What the life of an astronomer at the great telescopes is really like.
92. **The Scout Mindset** — Julia Galef — How to want to see things as they really are, not as you wish them to be.
93. **The Dawn of Everything** — David Graeber and David Wengrow — Early human societies were far more varied and experimental than the usual story says.
94. **Americanah** — Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie — Love, migration and race between Nigeria, the US and the UK.
95. **An Immense World** — Ed Yong — How other animals sense the world, and how little of it we perceive.
96. **Chip War** — Chris Miller — How the semiconductor became the world's most critical technology, and the contest to control it.
97. **Material World** — Ed Conway — Sand, salt, iron, copper, oil and lithium: the physical materials beneath the digital economy.
98. **Our Moon** — Rebecca Boyle — How the Moon shaped Earth, life and human history.
99. **Our Brains, Our Selves** — Masud Husain — A neurologist's patients show how brain changes can alter who we are. Winner of the 2025 Royal Society Trivedi Science Book Prize.
100. **Nexus** — Yuval Noah Harari — A history of information networks, from stone tablets to AI, and what they mean for power and truth.
